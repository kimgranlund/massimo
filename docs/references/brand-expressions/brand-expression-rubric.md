# Reading a brand expression slide

A brand expression board gathers examples of how a brand looks, speaks or behaves. Catalog each slide by what someone can learn from it. A contact sheet may contain many slides, and a slide may contain several **content islands**.

## Six questions for any slide

| Question | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Slide intent · What judgment should this slide teach, and why does that judgment matter in this design discipline? | Name the human reason for making the choice deliberately. Connect it to what a viewer can notice, feel, understand or remember. The intent should still hold if the slide is laid out differently. |
| 2. Strategic value · What does this particular brand stand to gain by making that choice? | Connect the intended outcome to a broader consequence. Point to a visible choice that makes the connection plausible. Phrase an inferred benefit as an interpretation, rather than claiming a measured result. |
| 3. What does this slide show or demonstrate? | Name the subject and what the viewer can observe. Useful verbs include introduces, compares, constructs, sequences and applies. “Shows how a type family handles headlines, amounts and labels” tells a reader more than “shows type.” |
| 4. What category does it demonstrate? | Choose the topic someone would search for to find this example. Common categories include Brand, Color, Typography, Geometry, Motion, Layout, Imagery, Voice and Application. Choose one primary category. Use Brand for a slide about identity or positioning and Application for one about how the identity works in a particular setting. A secondary category is useful when the slide also teaches something about that topic. |
| 5. Practical value · What decision or comparison does it help with? | Name a decision the slide helps someone make, a distinction it clarifies or a comparison it makes possible. If the answer repeats the description, finish this sentence instead. “After seeing this, a designer can decide ___ because ___.” |
| 6. What content islands are shown? | List the groups a viewer can point to, in reading order. An island is a group with one job, such as a swatch family, a construction drawing, a sequence of frames or a set of usage examples. Keep a specimen and its caption together. Treat repeated examples as one island when they invite a single comparison. |

**What makes a slide especially good?** Identify the choice that earns the slide a place in the collection. One image can do this on its own. For a slide with several islands, try mentally removing one. On D07, removing the usage strip preserves the palette but loses the guidance about proportion. That tells you precisely what the strip contributes.

## Controlled language rules

1. Use the same six fields in order: Slide intent, Strategic value, Demonstration, Category, Practical value and Content islands. Preserve the printed slide title and use a stable catalog term for its category.
2. Name the judgment the slide teaches and why it matters to a person. Explain what someone can notice, understand, feel or remember.
3. State visible evidence in the present tense. Use verbs such as *pairs*, *aligns* and *assigns*. Keep numbers attached to their units and scope. Use *can* for an inferred strategic benefit. Claim an achieved audience response only when there is evidence of that response.
4. Give each slide one primary category. Name a practical decision and the example that helps a reader make it.
5. Give content islands concrete names in reading order. Describe each with an instruction naming its placement, contents or relationship to another island.
6. Use an adjective or metaphor when it clarifies a design choice. Tie “loud” blue to the area of the field and the surrounding neutrals.

The [Halfkey manifest index](Halfkey/manifests/README.md) applies these rules to every book page and App Store panel. [Book D / 07 Colour](Halfkey/manifests/d07.json) is the worked color spec.

## Finding the why

**Color.** Ask what repeated encounters with the color could teach someone. On a sign, color can orient a viewer before they read. Across years, the same hue can become a point of recognition or carry a connection to a brand’s heritage. Its emotional effect depends on context, contrast and how boldly it is used. Look for the reason this slide supports. A new palette can aim to build equity over time even when it has no inherited associations.

**Typography.** Think of choosing a typeface as choosing the instruments for a piece of music. The words supply the phrase, while the face gives it a timbre. Weight, size and spacing determine which part leads and which accompanies. Ask how the reader should hear these particular words, and what relationship the typography establishes between them.
